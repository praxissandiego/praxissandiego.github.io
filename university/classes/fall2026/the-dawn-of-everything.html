Course Description

The Dawn Of Everything: A Guided Reading -- Part 2

We've heard the official story: humans started out as innocent, egalitarian foragers. Then somebody invented farming and the rest was inevitable: cities, kings, bureaucracies, and the 60-hour work week. Hierarchy, exploitation and inequality, we're told, are just the price of civilization.

But what if this was just a soothing fairytale lulling us into passivity, cutting us off from creative, historical possibilities?

Drawing on decades of new archaeology and anthropology, The Dawn of Everything tears up the script and reveals a human past far stranger, freer, and more politically inventive than we imagined: cities that ran for centuries with no rulers, societies that rearranged their entire power structure with the seasons, and Indigenous thinkers whose sharp critiques of European inequality may have lit the fuse on of the Enlightenment itself.

This isn't nostalgia for some lost utopia. Instead, it asks: if inequality wasn't inflicted on us by farming, or cities, or population growth, or any of the usual suspects… how did we get stuck? How did we lose the freedom our ancestors took for granted: the freedom to walk away, to disobey, to imagine the whole arrangement differently and then actually rebuild it.

This is a slow, intentional, communal read. No prior background needed. Just bring your curiosity and a willingness to have some assumptions cheerfully dismantled. We'll go chapter by chapter, argue in good faith, and ask the question the book keeps circling back to: if humans have built nearly every imaginable kind of society, why do we act like we're stuck with this one?

Come get your political imagination back.

Syllabus

Disclaimer: this is a long book. This class will continue from where we finished the first term. We will read 5 chapters, 250 pages. At a pace of about one-two chapters every two weeks. You are not required to complete the reading to attend the class, but it greatly improves the discussion. Schedule below.

My page numbers are based on the hardcover, which you can also purchase. Alternatively, there are also many free PDFs available online. Simply search “Dawn of Everything PDF” and you’ll easily find them. Let me know if you need help accessing the reading.

Schedule:

Week 1: Chapter 8 and Chapter 9 ~85 pages
"Imaginary Cities: Eurasia's First Urbanites—in Mesopotamia, the Indus Valley, Ukraine and China—and How They Built Cities Without Kings"
The world's earliest cities repeatedly reached enormous scale without kings, palaces, or ruling elites—often governing themselves from the bottom up—which shatters the assumption that large populations must inevitably submit to centralized hierarchy.

"Hiding in Plain Sight: The Indigenous Origins of Social Housing and Democracy in the Americas"
The same story plays out across the Atlantic: Teotihuacan rebuilt itself around decent housing for ordinary people, and Tlaxcala ran a genuine participatory democracy—so self-rule at urban scale, and democracy itself, may be far older and more Indigenous than the Greek-origin myth allows.

Week 2: Chapter 10 -- "Why the State Has No Origin: The Humble Beginnings of Sovereignty, Bureaucracy and Politics" ~82 pages
"The state" isn't one thing with one origin—it's a late, contingent bolting-together of three separate forms of domination (control of violence, control of knowledge, and personal charisma), which have appeared alone and in every combination throughout history. Chasing the origin of the state is chasing a phantasm—and that bundle now seems to be coming apart around us.

Week 3: Chapter 11 -- "Full Circle: On the Historical Foundations of the Indigenous Critique" ~55 pages
The free, deliberative peoples who produced the Indigenous critique weren't "natural" egalitarians. As the deliberate abandonment of Cahokia suggests, they were heirs to a civilization that had known top-down power and consciously walked away from it—which means equality was a hard-won political achievement, and that is exactly what makes the critique worth taking seriously.

Week 4: Chapter 12 -- "Conclusion: The Dawn of Everything" + Class Wrap-Up ~26 pages
If our ancestors built nearly every imaginable kind of society and changed their minds, repeatedly, on purpose, then how did we end up stuck with this one? The conclusion gathers the three primordial freedoms—to move, to disobey, to remake our social world—asks how we lost them, and insists the loss was neither inevitable nor irreversible.
